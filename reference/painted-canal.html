How the painting works

Three layers are redrawn on every frame.

The hidden scene. A small off-screen canvas, about 360 pixels wide, draws a plain version of the view: the sky gradient, aurora curtains built from layered sine waves, the distant skyline, the buildings, the gondola and the lanterns. The water is the sky flipped upside down, with each row nudged sideways to make ripples.

The brushstrokes. You never see that scene directly. Each frame, around a thousand curved strokes land at random spots on the visible canvas. Every stroke takes its color from the hidden scene and its direction from where it lands: tall streaks in the aurora, long flat strokes on the water, short dabs on the walls. Strokes shrink near edges so windows and rooftops stay readable. New strokes keep covering old ones, so the painting follows the moving scene with a soft lag, like wet paint.

The light. A third layer adds what paint can’t hold: twinkling stars, flickering lamplight, lantern glow and sun glints on the water, blended on top so they stay bright. A canvas-weave texture and a vignette sit over everything.

For the real site, the stroke pass can move into a WebGL shader with React Three Fiber so the GPU paints far more strokes, with this canvas version kept as the fallback and a still frame for anyone who prefers reduced motion.
